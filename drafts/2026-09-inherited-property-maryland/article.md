# You Just Inherited a House in Maryland. Here's What Actually Happens Next.

Losing a parent or family member is hard enough without also becoming a
part-time real estate and tax expert overnight. If you've inherited a
house in Maryland — alone or with siblings — here's what actually matters
before you decide whether to sell, and how it plays out financially.

## The tax break most people don't know they have

When you inherit a property, your cost basis for tax purposes isn't what
the original owner paid decades ago — it "steps up" to the home's fair
market value on the date of death. If your parent bought the house for
$80,000 in 1985 and it's worth $450,000 today, your basis is $450,000, not
$80,000. Sell it soon after inheriting at close to that value, and your
taxable capital gain can be minimal or close to zero — a real, significant
tax advantage that catches a lot of heirs pleasantly by surprise.

## Maryland's inheritance tax — a wrinkle most states don't have

Maryland is one of only a handful of states with an inheritance tax, and
it's the only state with both an inheritance tax and an estate tax. The
good news for most families: spouses, children, parents, and other direct
lineal relatives are generally exempt. More distant relatives and
non-relatives can face a flat rate — commonly cited around 10% — on what
they inherit, though exact classification (for example, exactly how
siblings are treated) can vary by circumstance, so this is worth
confirming with an estate attorney or tax professional rather than
assuming, especially if the estate involves anyone outside your immediate
family.

## Probate — expect it to take a while

If the property goes through Maryland's Orphans' Court probate process
(rather than being held in a trust that avoids it), expect the timeline
to be measured in months, not weeks — especially if there's no will, or
if there are multiple heirs who need to agree on next steps. This is
often the part that surprises people most: you can't necessarily list and
sell the day after the funeral, even if you want to.

## If you're splitting it with siblings

Multiple heirs inheriting one property is common, and it's also where
things get complicated fastest. You'll generally need agreement among all
heirs to sell, and if one sibling wants to keep the house and buy the
others out, that buyout price is often based on a professional appraisal
— not the last tax assessment, which can be significantly lower than
actual market value. Getting an independent appraisal early, before
anyone gets attached to a number, tends to prevent a lot of family
friction later.

## Sell as-is, or fix it up first?

Inherited homes are often older, sometimes deferred-maintenance-heavy,
and heirs are frequently spread across different states or simply want
closure quickly. Selling as-is is a completely legitimate path — but it's
worth getting a real opinion on whether light, targeted updates would
meaningfully change your net proceeds before assuming as-is is the better
financial choice. Sometimes it is; sometimes a $3,000 fix nets $20,000
more. That's worth a conversation, not a guess.

## What to actually do first

- Get the property professionally appraised before any sibling
  discussions about keeping vs. selling
- Talk to an estate attorney or tax professional about your specific
  inheritance tax exposure before assuming a number
- Understand where the estate stands in the probate process before
  committing to a listing timeline
- Get a straight opinion on as-is vs. light repairs before deciding —
  don't assume either answer

This is one of the most emotionally loaded transactions in real estate,
and also one where getting the financial and legal pieces right matters
enormously. I'd rather walk you through the whole picture up front than
have you find out the hard way what you didn't know.
