# What Nobody Tells You Before You Buy a Farm in Maryland

You found it. Twelve rolling acres, a white fence line, a farmhouse with
good bones, maybe a barn that's seen better days but has real potential.
It feels like the dream — until three things show up that nobody warned
you about: a tax you've never heard of, restrictions you didn't know
existed, and a financing conversation that doesn't sound anything like a
normal mortgage.

If you're considering a farm, equestrian property, or country estate in
Carroll County, here's what actually matters before you write an offer.

## The tax surprise most buyers never see coming

Maryland doesn't just charge the standard transfer and recordation tax on
farmland — it layers on a separate **Agricultural Land Transfer Tax**,
plus a 25% surcharge on top of that base tax. It applies specifically to
transfers of land assessed as agricultural, which most working farms and
larger rural parcels are. There are some narrow exemptions — for example,
a small parcel (2 acres or less, improved) transferred to a child or
grandchild — but for a typical arm's-length purchase, this is a real,
additional cost that a buyer coming from a standard suburban purchase
usually isn't budgeting for. It gets factored into closing costs, and it's
one of the first things worth running numbers on before you fall in love
with a property.

## Not all "farmland" is actually yours to do what you want with

This is the one that catches people off guard the most. A lot of farmland
in Carroll and the surrounding counties sits under a **Maryland
Agricultural Land Preservation Foundation (MALPF) easement** — a
permanent, recorded restriction that limits the land to agricultural or
woodland use. These easements aren't a quirk of the current owner's
choice; they're attached to the deed and bind every future owner,
including you.

Practically, that means: you generally can't subdivide the land, and any
change beyond what the easement already allows — including certain new
construction — typically needs approval from the Foundation itself, not
just the county. If you're buying 30 acres imagining a second home for
family, a wedding venue, or a future subdivision, the easement (if one
exists) may quietly take all of that off the table. This is disclosed in
title work, but it's worth asking about explicitly, in plain language,
before you're emotionally attached to a property.

## Well and septic aren't a line item — they're a deal point

On a rural property, water and wastewater aren't hooked up to county
systems — you're dealing with a private well and a septic system, and
both need real evaluation before you commit. In Maryland, well yield and
water quality testing, plus a septic evaluation following the state's
formal inspection procedure, are standard steps — and most lenders will
require them before they'll finance the property at all.

Budget for this as a real, non-negotiable part of due diligence, not an
afterthought. A failed septic system or a well with inadequate yield can
run into serious repair costs, and it's far better to know before closing
than after.

## Financing a farm isn't the same conversation as financing a subdivision house

If income and location line up, a **USDA Rural Development loan** can be
a genuinely strong option for buyers of eligible rural properties — no
down payment, competitive fixed rates, and it's built specifically for
areas like much of Carroll County. But it's designed around
primarily-residential properties for buyers under local income limits,
and if the property includes an active income-producing agricultural
operation, that can complicate both loan eligibility and how the
appraisal gets handled. This is a conversation to have with your lender
early — not something to assume works the same as a standard 30-year
conventional mortgage on a subdivision lot.

## A short due-diligence checklist

- Ask directly whether the property is under a MALPF or other agricultural
  preservation easement, and get the easement terms in writing
- Get well yield/water quality testing and a septic evaluation scheduled
  early — don't wait until the week before closing
- Run the numbers on the Agricultural Land Transfer Tax as part of your
  closing cost estimate, not as a surprise at the table
- Talk to a lender who's actually financed rural/agricultural property
  before, not just your usual go-to
- Confirm what outbuildings, fencing, and land use are actually included
  and permitted — verbally promised isn't the same as in the deed
