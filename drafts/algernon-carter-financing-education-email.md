Price cut vs. seller credit

For what you're trying to do, which is keep cash for renovations, I'd lean toward asking the seller for a credit toward your closing costs instead of a straight price cut.

The difference is this. Dropping the price from $329,000 to $319,000 mainly helps you over the life of the loan, with a smaller balance and a payment that's about $60 to $70 a month lower. It doesn't do much for the cash you actually bring to the closing table. A seller credit does, because it goes right against your closing costs and prepaids, so you show up owing less out of pocket and keep that money for the work you want to do.

Here's the comparison, using $13,000 as a rough placeholder for closing costs. Your lender's Loan Estimate will have the real number.

If we cut the price to $319K:
- Cash you bring to closing: about the same as now, a bit lower if you're putting money down
- Cash free for renovations: little change
- Loan balance: about $10K lower
- Monthly payment: about $65 a month lower
- Home has to appraise at: $319K, which is easier

If we ask for a $13K seller credit at $329K:
- Cash you bring to closing: much lower, potentially close to zero
- Cash free for renovations: up to about $13,000 more available right away
- Loan balance: about $10K higher
- Monthly payment: about $65 a month higher
- Home has to appraise at: $329K

Two trade-offs to know about with the credit. The house still has to appraise at $329,000, and if it comes in under that we're renegotiating anyway. And your balance and payment run a little higher than the price-cut version. We can also do a mix, a small price reduction plus a credit, which is often the easiest thing to get a seller to agree to.

Before we finalize, run these by your lender:
- Your actual cash to close both ways. Ask them to run a Loan Estimate with a $10K price reduction and another with a $10K to $13K seller credit so you can compare side by side.
- The most the seller is allowed to credit you on a VA loan, and whether the funding fee can be part of that.
- Whether their investor puts any cap on seller-paid costs.

Once you send me those two Loan Estimates, I'll write the offer around whichever one gets you where you want to be.

If I'm approved for $340K and buy at $320K, do I keep the $20K?

No. An approval is the ceiling your lender will go to based on your income and credit. It's not money sitting in an account. Buy at $320,000 and you borrow around $320,000, and there's no leftover to take. A regular purchase loan also can't have extra cash rolled into it for renovations, or for a boat or jet skis. The loan pays for the house and nothing else.

If you want renovation money built into the financing

There are loan products that combine the purchase and a renovation budget into one loan. FHA 203(k) and the conventional HomeStyle are the common ones, and some lenders do a VA renovation loan that keeps your VA terms. Each has its own down payment, mortgage insurance, and contractor and escrow rules, and the money goes to the contractors as the work gets done, not to you.

I'm not pushing you toward any of these, I just want you to know they exist. If it's a direction you want to explore, ask your lender:
- Do you offer a VA renovation loan, and what's the cap on the work?
- What would a 203(k) or HomeStyle cost me compared to my current VA approval, in down payment, monthly payment, and timeline?
- If I keep my current loan, what are my options for renovation cash later, like a home equity line or a renovation loan?

Whatever they come back with, send it my way and we'll work it into how we structure the deal.
