# New Construction or Resale? What You're Actually Choosing Between

"Should we just build new?" comes up in almost every buyer conversation
at some point. It's a real question with real trade-offs — not simply
new-is-better or old-has-character. Here's what you're actually weighing.

## The case for new construction

Everything is untouched — you're the first to use every appliance, every
system, every surface. That usually means low repair costs in the early
years, better energy efficiency than an older home, and often a builder's
warranty covering structural and mechanical issues for a defined period.
In a strong seller's market, new construction can also mean skipping the
bidding-war chaos entirely — you're negotiating with a builder, not
competing against ten other buyer offers on the same resale listing.

## The case against — where new construction gets people

Cost is the big one. Newly built homes typically carry a real price
premium over comparable resale homes, driven by materials, labor, and
lot scarcity — and that premium often shows up in property taxes too,
since new homes get assessed at their higher current value immediately.
Timelines can also stretch three to nine months or longer if you're
buying pre-construction, and delays are common, not rare. Lots tend to be
smaller with less mature landscaping, since builders maximize buildable
area on each parcel. And builders generally have very little room to
negotiate on price, even when they'll happily throw in upgrades or
closing-cost credits instead.

## The one thing most buyers don't realize: whose side is the builder's agent on?

The sales representative sitting in that gorgeous model home works for
the builder — not you. That doesn't mean they're dishonest, but it does
mean nobody in that room is representing your interests unless you bring
your own agent. Builders can't charge you more for using your own agent,
and can't require you to use their preferred lender — though they can and
often do offer incentives (rate buydowns, design credits, closing cost
coverage) specifically for buyers who use their in-house lender. That's
worth comparing against your own lender's numbers before assuming the
incentive is actually the better deal.

## The case for resale

You get mature trees, established neighborhoods, real square footage
per dollar in most markets, and — critically — you can actually see and
inspect the exact home you're buying, not a floor plan or a model down
the street. Negotiating room tends to be real, especially outside peak
season. The tradeoff: aging systems, deferred maintenance you may not
discover until you own it, and the genuine possibility of competing
against other offers.

## What to actually weigh

- Compare true monthly cost, not just sticker price — property taxes on
  new construction are often higher immediately, not phased in
- If a builder incentive requires their lender, get a competing quote
  from your own before deciding it's the better deal
- Bring your own agent to new construction from the first visit — it
  costs you nothing and changes who's actually looking out for you
- For resale, budget for what an inspection will likely find — new
  construction's biggest advantage is exactly what resale can't promise:
  a truly blank maintenance history

Neither choice is universally right. What's right depends on your
timeline, your tolerance for negotiating, and what you actually value —
and that's a conversation worth having before you fall for a model home
or a listing photo.
