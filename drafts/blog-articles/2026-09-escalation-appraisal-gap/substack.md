# The Clause That Wins Bidding Wars — And the One That Can Blow Up Your Deal

I've watched a buyer win the house and lose thirty grand in the same
afternoon. Same signature. Same closing table a month later. The winning
didn't cause the losing, exactly, but the two pieces of contract language
that won her the house are also what set up the check she wasn't expecting
to write, and nobody walked her through that part until it was too late to
change it.

Those two pieces of language are the escalation clause and the appraisal
gap. In a competitive Maryland offer they do most of the heavy lifting.
Almost everyone I sit down with has heard the names. Almost nobody can
tell me what they actually commit you to. So here's the plain version.

## The escalation clause, in plain terms

An escalation clause is language in your offer that automatically bumps
your bid above any competing offer, in fixed steps, up to a ceiling you
set. In Maryland it's not something your agent improvises. There's a
standard Maryland Realtors form for it, the Purchase Price Escalation
Addendum, and you submit it *with* your first offer, not after the seller
asks for highest and best.

It's three numbers. Your starting price. The step, or increment, which is
how much you jump above the next offer each time, usually somewhere in the
$1,000 to $5,000 range around here. And your cap, which is the absolute
most you'll pay, full stop.

Say you offer $525,000 with a $3,000 step and a $560,000 cap. Another
buyer comes in at $540,000. Your offer clicks up to $543,000 and stops,
nowhere near your cap. If that other buyer also had an escalation clause,
the two of you keep stepping up until somebody's cap runs out.

One thing that protects you: in Maryland the seller is supposed to show
proof of the competing offer that set yours off. If that proof never
appears, you don't just shrug and pay the higher number. Have your agent
ask for the competing contract, price and terms pages at least, before you
accept that your clause fired at all.

## The three numbers, and the one that's really your whole hand

The step is tactics. The starting price is negotiation. The cap is the one
that can quietly cost you, because the moment the seller sees it, they've
seen the top of what you'll pay for their house.

Some agents write the addendum so the seller only sees the current step,
not your ceiling, until the ceiling actually matters. Whether that's on
the table depends on how the offer's built. Ask before yours goes in.

And know this going in: an escalation clause does not win you the house. It
wins you the *price* argument. Maryland listing agents are coached to run
the net proceeds on every offer, not just eyeball the biggest number. A
clean offer five grand under your escalated bid, with a bigger deposit or
fewer contingencies or a faster close, beats you. Escalating keeps you from
losing on price alone. It does nothing if you're losing on terms.

## Now the other one: the appraisal gap

This one has nothing to do with your escalation clause. It shows up two or
three weeks after you're under contract, and it's a financing problem.

Your lender will only loan against the appraised value. So if you're under
contract at $525,000 and the appraisal comes back at $505,000, the bank
builds your loan on $505,000. That $20,000 difference is now cash you carry
into closing, on top of your down payment, if you still want the house at
your price.

Why does it happen? Appraisals lean on recent comparable sales, and in a
market that's moving, those comps trail what people are actually paying
today. That's the whole mechanism behind a house going well over asking and
still handing the buyer a five-figure bill to close the gap.

## Why these two end up glued together

To win, buyers escalate. To make the seller believe the deal will actually
close, buyers add appraisal gap coverage — a promise to cover a low
appraisal, up to some limit. Both promises get made the same afternoon,
before you know your final price *or* the appraised value.

Run it out. You escalate from $525,000 up to $548,000 to win. Three weeks
later the appraisal lands at $525,000, right where you started. If you
pledged to cover up to $25,000 of a gap, you now owe the full $23,000 in
cash, because your escalated price outran the appraisal. By your own math
you didn't overpay. You just made two promises about numbers you couldn't
see yet, and they moved against you.

## The move that actually protects you

There's a difference between *capping* your gap coverage and *waiving the
appraisal contingency*, and people mix them up constantly.

Keep the appraisal contingency and a low number gives you options —
renegotiate, or walk. Waive it entirely and your exposure is open-ended;
whatever the gap is, it's yours. A gap coverage clause is the middle: you
tell the seller you'll cover a shortfall up to a set amount you picked on
purpose. "Buyer pays up to $15,000 over a low appraisal." "Buyer and
seller split the difference up to $Y each." Competitive, but your downside
has a floor you chose.

Whatever you do, the planning number is the same. Set aside somewhere
around 2 to 5% of the price in cash you could actually put toward a gap,
separate from your down payment, before you write anything.

And if the appraisal does come in low and you're short — ask your lender
for a reconsideration of value with better comps, look at shifting your
down payment percentage to free up cash, or go back to the seller, who
often meets you partway rather than re-listing.

## What I'd tell you before you write it

The buyers who come through this fine aren't the ones who offer the most.
They're the ones who knew both numbers — their real ceiling and their real
cash on hand — before anyone was in a bidding war. Set your cap before
you're emotional about a specific house. Set your gap coverage limit at or
below the cash you can genuinely bring. Ask how your escalation addendum
handles disclosure. Know which contingency you're keeping.

That's a twenty-minute conversation, and it's worth having before you're
attached to an address, not after.
