# The Clause That Wins Bidding Wars — And the One That Can Blow Up Your Deal

You've found the house. So has everyone else. In a competitive Maryland
market, two pieces of contract language decide more offers than almost
anything else: the escalation clause and the appraisal gap. Understand
both before you write your next offer, because used wrong, either one can
cost you the house — or a lot more cash than you planned to spend.

## What an escalation clause actually does

An escalation clause is language added to your offer that automatically
increases your bid above any competing offer, by a set increment, up to a
maximum price you name. It only activates if the seller has proof of
another bona fide competing offer — your agent can (and should) ask for
that proof before assuming it triggered.

A typical clause has three parts: proof of a competing offer, an
escalation increment (say, $2,000 above the next offer), and a hard price
cap — the absolute most you're willing to pay. If another buyer also
submits an escalation clause, both trigger and the price climbs until one
buyer's cap is reached.

The risk: a seller who sees your cap number has effectively seen your
whole hand. Some agents write the clause to reveal only the current
winning bid, not your ceiling, until it's actually needed — worth
discussing with whoever's writing your offer.

## What an appraisal gap actually is

An appraisal gap is the difference between your offer price and what the
lender's appraiser says the home is actually worth. Since most lenders
will only finance up to the appraised value, that gap becomes cash you
have to bring to the table yourself if you still want the deal to close
at your offer price.

In a competitive market, appraisals — based on recent comparable sales —
can lag behind what buyers are actually willing to pay, especially when
demand is outrunning what recent comps reflect. That's how a home can sell
well over asking and still leave the buyer owing tens of thousands in
appraisal-gap cash.

## Why buyers pair the two — and where it gets risky

To compete, buyers increasingly promise upfront to cover part or all of
any appraisal gap in cash — before they even know how large that gap will
be. That's the real risk: agreeing to cover a gap you can't yet size is a
very different commitment than agreeing to a purchase price. Before you
make that promise, know your real number — how much extra cash you can
actually bring beyond your down payment if the appraisal comes in low.

## What to actually do before you offer

- Set your true price ceiling before you see any competing numbers — not
  in the moment, under pressure
- Know your available cash beyond the down payment before promising to
  cover any appraisal gap
- Ask your agent whether the escalation clause needs to reveal your full
  cap, or just the current top bid
- Get real, current guidance from someone who's actually written
  escalation and appraisal-gap language for other buyers in this specific
  market — not a generic template

These tools can absolutely win you a home in a competitive market. They
can also commit you to more than you meant to spend if you don't know
your real limits going in. That's the conversation to have with me before
you write the offer, not after.
