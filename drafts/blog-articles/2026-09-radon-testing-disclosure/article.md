# The Gas You Can't See, Smell, or Taste — and What Maryland Actually Requires About It

Radon rarely comes up until an inspector mentions it, and by then most
buyers have never heard of it. Here's what it actually is, what Maryland
does and doesn't require around it, and why it's worth testing for
regardless of what the paperwork says.

## What radon actually is

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas, produced by the decay of
uranium in soil and rock. It's colorless, odorless, and tasteless — the
only way to know your levels is to test. It seeps up from the ground into
homes through foundation cracks, sump pits, and other openings, and it's
recognized as a leading cause of lung cancer in non-smokers. None of that
means every home has a radon problem — but you genuinely cannot tell by
looking, smelling, or how the house feels.

## What Maryland actually requires — and what it doesn't

Some states (Minnesota, Colorado, and others) have passed radon-specific
disclosure statutes that require sellers to test and disclose results in
a dedicated form. Maryland does not have that kind of dedicated,
radon-specific statute. What Maryland does require is the general
Residential Property Disclosure/Disclaimer Statement under the state's
Real Property Article — sellers choosing the "disclosure" option (rather
than selling strictly "as-is") must reveal known material defects,
which would include radon test results they're actually aware of. But
critically: Maryland does not require sellers to test for radon in the
first place. No test means no known results to disclose — which means a
completely clean-looking disclosure form tells you nothing about whether
anyone has ever actually checked.

## What that means practically

Don't treat a disclosure form with no radon mentions as reassurance. It
may simply mean nobody's tested. If radon matters to you — and given the
health stakes, it should — the responsible move is to test independently
during your own due diligence period, the same way you'd get a home
inspection, rather than relying on what a seller may or may not know.

## The good news: it's genuinely fixable

Elevated radon isn't a dealbreaker. Mitigation systems — typically a vent
pipe and fan system that redirects gas from beneath the foundation
outside the home — generally run in the low thousands of dollars
depending on the home's size and layout, and they work well. This is a
manageable negotiation point, not a reason to walk from an otherwise
great house.

## What to actually do

- Don't assume "no radon mentioned" on a disclosure form means the home
  was tested — ask directly whether it was
- Build radon testing into your due diligence period like you would a
  home inspection, especially for homes with basements
- If levels come back elevated, treat it as a negotiation point (repair
  credit or seller-paid mitigation), not a reason to abandon the deal
- Sellers: getting ahead of this with your own test before listing can
  prevent a late surprise during a buyer's due diligence window

This is one of those things nobody thinks about until it's suddenly the
only thing anyone's talking about at the kitchen table mid-contract. Better
to know early.
