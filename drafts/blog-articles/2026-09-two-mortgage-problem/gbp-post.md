## LinkedIn

Half the move-up buyers I talk to never make it to an offer.

Not because they can't afford the next house. They can. It's the in-between that stops them: how do you buy the next place without being on the hook for two mortgages while your current one sells?

There are three ways around it, and which one fits has more to do with your timeline than your bank account:

1. Sell first, then rent your home back from the buyer for up to ~59 days. Your equity is real cash before you shop, and you're not homeless the day you close.

2. Buy first by borrowing against your current home — a bridge loan (8.5–11%), a HELOC (7–9.5%, but you have to set it up before you list), or an 80-10-10. You pay for the certainty, but you don't lose the house you actually want.

3. Write a contingent offer. It still works in slower pockets, but in Carroll, Howard, and Frederick County right now it's the weakest hand at the table.

None of them are perfect. The right call comes down to how much uncertainty you can live with — and that's a conversation worth having before the sign goes in the yard, not after.

## Google Business Profile — long version (~1,480 characters)

Buying a bigger home in Maryland but don't want two mortgages at once?

It's the question that stalls most move-up buyers across Carroll, Howard, Frederick, and Baltimore County — Westminster, Eldersburg, Mount Airy, Ellicott City and beyond: how do you buy your next Maryland home before your current one sells, without paying two loans at the same time?

Three real options, with rough 2026 costs:

1) Sell first, then rent your home back from the buyer for up to about 59 days. Your equity becomes cash before you shop, and your next offer can skip the home-sale contingency. Cheapest, cleanest route.

2) Buy first by borrowing against your current home — a bridge loan (about 8.5–11%), a HELOC (about 7–9.5%, opened before you list), or an 80-10-10 loan (6.5–8% blended). You pay 2–11% for the certainty of locking down a house before another buyer does.

3) Make a contingent offer. Still works in slower pockets, but it's the weakest offer in a tight market, and it usually comes with a 48–72 hour kick-out clause.

With the 30-year fixed mortgage rate near 6.71% and Maryland inventory still tight, which path fits comes down to your timeline and how much uncertainty you can handle — not your bank balance.
